Geospatial Jobs Cluster Where Federal Money Moves

The US labor market in October 2026 is stalled rather than sinking: unemployment has held between 4.1% and 4.3% since March, payrolls grew just 29,000 in September against a 12-month average of about 45,000, and the Fed hiked rates in September into 3.4% inflation, producing a "low-hire, low-fire" regime in which layoffs and jobless claims stay low but hiring plans are at a 15-year low. Spatially, growth is concentrated in Texas (+154,300 jobs over the year), North Carolina, South Carolina, Louisiana and Utah, while the Washington DC region is the one large metro in outright contraction (-66,000 jobs, -2.0%) because of 2025 federal workforce cuts. For geospatial science, that federal reshuffle matters more than the national headline: the core geospatial occupations are small (roughly 14,700 cartographers/photogrammetrists, 52,000 surveyors and 1,500 geographers), about half their jobs sit in government, and 2025–26 moved federal GEOINT work out of Washington toward St. Louis (the $1.7 billion Next NGA West campus opened October 2025 with about 3,150 NGA staff) and Huntsville (Space Command and 500 FBI positions inbound). Weighing employment base, pay after housing costs, employer momentum and a Missouri student's access to clearance pipelines, the ranked recommendations are: St. Louis first (best home-price-to-wage ratio of any hub at 3.7x and the only growing federal GEOINT anchor), then Denver/Front Range (660 cartographer jobs, the highest post-rent pay of any metro at about $73,400), Washington DC/Northern Virginia for cleared defense-intelligence work only, Huntsville, Raleigh-Durham, Austin/Houston, Kansas City and the coastal spatial-data-science markets (Bay Area, Seattle) for those on a programming track. The biggest caveat is data vintage: metro-level geospatial employment and wage figures are May 2023 OEWS (the newest static tables retrievable), rents are October 2026, and no metro-level 2026 geospatial job-posting counts exist in any source, so the rankings rest on structural anchors and 2025–26 employer events rather than on a live posting index.

Payrolls average 45,000 a month: a low-hire, low-fire stall

The September 2026 Employment Situation set the tone for the autumn. Nonfarm payrolls rose by 29,000, well under the roughly 90,000 consensus, and the July and August figures were revised down by a combined 60,000, turning July into a net-loss month (-10,000); over the prior twelve months payroll growth averaged only about 45,000 per month (BLS Employment Situation, Sep 2026; iHire summary). Unemployment ticked up to 4.2% (7.1 million unemployed) but has not left the 4.1–4.3% band since March, partly because 485,000 people entered the labor force in September and not all found work at once; participation (61.8%) and the employment-population ratio (59.2%) have been flat since January (BLS). The margins are weakening even where the headline is not: Black unemployment jumped a full point to 7.0%, teen unemployment is 14.5%, and 1.9 million people (27.1% of the unemployed) have been out of work 27 weeks or more (BLS).

The JOLTS data explain why this feels stuck rather than collapsing. Openings fell to 7.1 million (4.3% rate) in August, hires were 5.2 million, and the quits rate sat at 1.9%, unchanged and low, while layoffs stayed at 1.0% (BLS JOLTS, Aug 2026). Initial jobless claims were still only 197,000 in early October, and Challenger's September announced cuts (43,281) were the lowest for any September since 2022, with year-to-date cuts down 39% from 2025's DOGE-inflated pace (Admiral Markets; TheStreet Pro on Challenger). The flip side is that September hiring plans (90,787) were the lowest for the month since 2011 and the holiday hiring surge did not materialize (TheStreet Pro). U.S. Bank's Matt Schoeppner summarized it as employers still having positions to fill while "both workers and businesses appear increasingly reluctant to make major moves" (iHire).

Wages are losing ground. Average hourly earnings rose 3.0% year over year to $37.81, below August's 3.4% CPI (energy CPI +16.3%), so real pay is falling for the typical worker; ADP's data show job-changers still earn a premium (base pay +4.8% vs +3.0% for stayers), but the 1.9% quits rate means few are collecting it (BLS; ADP via Finviz). The October 14 Beige Book reported wage growth as merely moderate-to-slight across districts, with Boston staffing contacts noting that raises often lagged inflation (Federal Reserve Beige Book, Oct 14 2026). Monetary policy is now a headwind: according to broker commentary that should be checked against Fed statements, the FOMC raised the funds rate 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00% in September, the first hike since 2023, and the 10-year Treasury yield has traded above 5%, its highest since 2002, with markets pricing a further hike by December at roughly 75–85% odds (Admiral Markets). A Fed tightening into sub-50,000 payroll growth is an unusual, stagflation-adjacent mix, and it puts rate-sensitive sectors (construction, finance, real estate, state and local government) at the greatest risk of tipping from flat to negative over the next two quarters.

By sector, health care remains the engine but is decelerating sharply (+17,000 in September versus a +33,000 twelve-month average, and health-care openings fell 115,000 in August), manufacturing is in a modest rebound (+72,000 since a December 2025 low), construction is steady at about +10,000 a month, and financial activities (-129,000 since May 2025), information (-10,000), professional and business services (-9,000) and government (-17,000) are shedding (BLS; BLS JOLTS). Technology is the single largest source of announced layoffs (165,925 year-to-date, 29% of all cuts) while also being a top announced-hiring sector, which reads as churn toward AI-related roles rather than collapse; AI was cited in about 21% of 2026 cuts but its share has receded since spring (TheStreet Pro). Goldman Sachs estimates job growth in the most AI-exposed industries is running about 10,000 a month below trend, roughly offset by data-center construction hiring, and that only 23% of establishments have built AI into regular workflows; its central warning is that AI displacement "accelerates during downturns," so a recession, not the current expansion, is when deferred cuts would land (Fortune on Goldman Sachs). For a new graduate the relevant number is older but sobering: the New York Fed put recent-college-graduate unemployment at 5.6% as of March 2026, above the national rate (Bloomberg Law).

Texas, the Carolinas and Utah grow while Washington sheds 66,000 jobs

States: the South and Mountain West pull ahead

The state map for the year to September 2026 is a South and Mountain West story. Only seven states posted statistically significant payroll gains: Texas +154,300, California +129,300, North Carolina +67,000 and Minnesota +36,800 led in numbers, while South Carolina (+2.3%), Louisiana (+1.8%) and Utah (+1.5%) led in percentage terms; the District of Columbia was the only jurisdiction with a significant decline (-25,800, -3.5%) (BLS State Employment and Unemployment, Sep 2026). Unemployment is lowest in South Dakota (2.0%) and North Dakota (2.2%) and highest in DC (5.6%), California (5.1%) and Michigan (5.0%); Missouri's rate fell 0.3–0.4 point over the year, one of 20 states with significant declines, while Connecticut (+0.9), Alabama (+0.7), Oklahoma, Arizona, New Mexico and Minnesota saw rates rise (BLS LAUS). Regional openings data agree: the South had the highest openings rate (4.5%, rising) in August, the Midwest 4.4%, the West 4.1% (after the largest one-month drop, -243,000) and the Northeast 3.9% (BLS JOLTS).

Metros and counties: nine winners, five losers, 373 flat

The metro data make the stall vivid. Among 387 metros, payroll employment rose significantly over the year to August 2026 in only nine, fell in five, and was essentially unchanged in 373 (BLS Metropolitan Area Employment, Aug 2026). The numeric winners were Houston (+44,700), Minneapolis-St. Paul (+27,800) and Charlotte (+21,400); the percentage leaders were Baton Rouge and Myrtle Beach (+3.2% each) and Salt Lake City (+2.5%), with Raleigh-Cary (+2.2%) and San Jose (+1.6%) the other large metros in significant growth. The losers were Washington-Arlington-Alexandria (-66,000, -2.0%), Portland (-18,800, -1.5%) and Atlantic City, with Bloomington IN (-5.9%) and Flagstaff (-3.6%) the worst in percentage terms (BLS Metro). Unemployment among the 56 large metros ranged from Cleveland's 1.8% to Fresno's 7.8%, with Detroit (+1.0 point) and Chicago (5.2%, +1.1 point in its core division) the most notable deteriorations (BLS Metro).

County-level QCEW data (March 2026, the newest available) sharpen the picture and expose a Sunbelt split. National county employment was essentially flat (+0.1%) with weekly wages up 3.9%, and the fastest-growing large counties were Licking OH (+3.6%, Intel/Columbus data-center corridor), Monterey CA (+3.4%), Baldwin AL (+2.9%), Loudoun VA (+2.8%, the data-center capital), New Hanover NC (+2.4%) and Wake and Cabarrus NC (+2.3%), with Ada ID (Boise) at +2.1% and wages +7.9% (BLS QCEW county release, Aug 28 2026). The bottom of the 370-county ranking is a Washington map: DC and Arlington VA (-4.5% each), Montgomery MD (-4.0%), Harford MD (-3.0%), Prince George's MD (-2.9%), Frederick MD and Alexandria VA (-2.6%) and Fairfax VA (-2.1%), joined by Midland TX (oil, -2.7%), Lucas OH (-2.7%), Washington OR (Intel Hillsboro, -2.8%) and the military-base counties Greene OH (Wright-Patterson, -2.5%) and Cumberland NC (-2.2%) (BLS QCEW). Crucially, several Sunbelt cores show population inflow without job growth: Harris TX (-0.6%), Maricopa AZ (-0.5%), Travis TX (+0.2%) and Davidson TN (-0.3%) were flat or negative while their suburbs (Fort Bend, Collin, Montgomery and Hays TX; Cabarrus and Union NC; Hamilton IN; Douglas CO) posted the positive prints (BLS QCEW). Missouri's counties sit in the flat band: Greene (Springfield) +0.6% with wages up only 1.4%, Boone (Columbia) +0.1%, St. Charles +0.2%, Jackson (Kansas City) -0.7%, St. Louis County -0.8% and St. Louis City -1.1% (BLS QCEW).

Population, projects and sector mix explain the pattern

Population momentum underlies the Sunbelt-suburban pattern, but it slowed sharply: the US grew just 0.5% in 2024–25 (1.78 million) versus 1.0% the year before as net international migration halved, and counties of a million or more lost 637,634 residents to domestic out-migration while mid-sized counties gained 533,766 (Census Bureau, Mar 26 2026). Houston (+126,720) and Dallas-Fort Worth (+123,557) added the most people, followed by Atlanta, Phoenix, Charlotte (+54,122), Austin and, notably, Washington DC (+50,206, even as its jobs fell); the fastest-growing metros of any size were Ocala, Myrtle Beach, Spartanburg, Huntsville (+2.6%), Wilmington NC, St. George UT and Raleigh (+2.4%), and 45 of the 50 fastest-growing counties were in the South, while Los Angeles County lost 53,934 people (Census Bureau).

Capital investment is overwhelmingly flowing to data centers and semiconductors, which create far fewer permanent jobs per dollar than factories. McGuireWoods tallied more than $186 billion of 2025 data-center announcements against about $1.2 billion of all other project types in its tracked deals, J.P. Morgan expects hyperscalers to spend about $697 billion in 2026, and planned US data-center projects rose 203% between March and August 2026, led by Virginia, Texas, Georgia, Ohio and Indiana (McGuireWoods Consulting; Construction Dive). The scale-versus-jobs gap is stark: Google's $17 billion Cheyenne data center lists 250 jobs, while Pirelli's $1.2 billion Georgia tire plant lists 1,000 and Northrop Grumman's Layton, Utah expansion 1,500 (Site Selection Impact Report, Sept 2026). The largest 2026 construction starts include TSMC Arizona ($165 billion), Brightline West rail ($21 billion), Calcasieu Pass LNG in Louisiana ($15.1 billion) and Amazon's $15 billion Indiana data-center expansion (Newsweek). For a geospatial professional the relevant insight is that siting, permitting, utility and fiber mapping, and construction-phase surveying for this build-out are concentrated in Northern Virginia, central Ohio, Texas, Georgia and Indiana, which is why Loudoun and Licking counties lead the QCEW table.

Sector concentration explains the losers. The Washington metro holds 17% of all non-military federal jobs, and roughly 200,000 federal positions were cut nationally in 2025, with the pain spilling into suburban Virginia (48% of DC-area federal employees live there) and Maryland (40%) (Oxford Economics). The AI-tech economy, by contrast, is both growing and spatially concentrated: CBRE counts 98,699 AI workers in the San Francisco Bay Area, 67,949 in New York, 41,591 in Seattle, 33,515 in Washington and 29,840 in Dallas-Fort Worth; AI-skilled tech talent grew 45% in a year while broader tech talent grew 1.8%, and AI now accounts for 31% of US tech job listings (57% in the Bay Area) (CBRE Scoring Tech Talent 2026). Huntsville ranked first among CBRE's "opportunity markets" for the fifth straight year with 25,910 tech workers (+18% in three years) and an average tech wage of $123,142, and LinkedIn's 2026 Cities on the Rise list is led by Augusta GA (cyber, defense, data centers), Richmond VA and Reno (Axios Huntsville; LinkedIn News).

Housing cost cuts across all of this. A median-income family now needs 36% of income for a median existing home at a 6.5% mortgage rate; San Jose (82% of income), San Francisco (71%) and San Diego (68%) are the least affordable metros, while Pittsburgh, Cleveland and Oklahoma City require only about $71,500–$73,700 of income to buy the median home (NAHB Eye on Housing; HSH.com 2Q26). Among tech markets, CBRE finds rent-to-tech-wage ratios most favorable in San Antonio (12.3%), Austin (12.7%) and Raleigh-Durham (13.5%) (CBRE). Affordability and job growth are largely inversely related in 2026 except in the Carolinas, Texas and Utah, which is the main reason those three regions keep appearing in the recommendations below.

Core geospatial occupations hold 70,000 jobs, but graduates land in adjacent roles

The occupational statistics for geospatial work are small and must be read as a floor. BLS counts about 14,700 cartographers and photogrammetrists (median $81,390 in May 2025, projected +7% over 2025–35, about 900 openings a year), 52,000 surveyors (median $75,440, +6%, about 3,100 openings a year, licensure required in every state) and just 1,500 geographers (median $102,040, -2%, roughly 100 replacement openings a year) (BLS OOH, Cartographers; BLS OOH, Surveyors; BLS OOH, Geographers). Against that, universities awarded 7,105 geography and cartography credentials in 2024 (3,577 of them specifically in GIS and cartography), so the two "pure" geospatial SOC codes absorb only about one graduate in seven (Data USA, Geography & Cartography; Data USA, GIS & Cartography). The real market is the adjacent pool: data scientists (275,600 jobs, median $120,230, +35% growth, about 24,800 openings a year), surveying and mapping technicians, planners, software developers and the catch-all "computer occupations, all other" code where ONET files GIS technologists (27,000 projected annual openings for the parent code, which ONET flags as "Bright Outlook") (BLS OOH, Data Scientists; O*NET 15-1299.02). A geospatial data scientist median of about $117,250 versus roughly $75,000 for a traditional GIS analyst is the gap that programming skills close (GeoSpatial Training Services, Jan 2026).

Government is the geographic anchor. Local government holds 41% of cartographer jobs and the federal government pays them best ($109,300 median versus $81,390 overall); 64% of geographers work for the federal government at a $110,010 median; and the government surveyor median ($94,970) tops engineering services ($73,320), where 71% of surveyors work (BLS OOH, Cartographers; BLS OOH, Geographers; BLS OOH, Surveyors). That dependence is why the 2025 federal cuts hit this field harder than the headline economy: the geographer code is effectively a federal occupation (Maryland location quotient 10.95, Washington metro LQ 9.54 in May 2023, consistent with NGA, Census and USGS employment), and its projected decline is better explained by federal headcount than by AI (BLS OEWS May 2023, 19-3092). Recruiters reported the consequence in 2025: Geosearch's Jessica Touchard described "a dramatic downturn in certain sectors," cuts to federally funded projects, laid-off federal staff entering the market, and postings on her site drawing 300-plus applicants within a day or two, even as WGIC's Aaron Addison put openings at 20,000–25,000 and predicted they would double in five years (Geo Week News, May 2025). Both can be true: the structural narrative (geospatial market CAGR of 11–14%, graduate supply growing about 2% a year) coexists with a cyclical, policy-driven slump concentrated in federal-adjacent work (FIG 2025, Addison).

Where the core jobs physically are, by the newest retrievable metro tables (May 2023), is a short list. Cartographer and photogrammetrist employment leads in Washington DC (700 jobs, mean $94,100), Denver (660, LQ 5.12, $92,780), Houston (430), Austin (370, LQ 3.69), Dallas-Fort Worth and Portland (330 each), Seattle (320, $95,370), Los Angeles (270), Phoenix and Raleigh (240 each, Raleigh LQ 4.15); the highest-paying metros are San Jose ($125,600), San Francisco ($117,630), Sacramento ($112,190) and, anomalously, Dayton ($101,280 on 40 jobs, a Wright-Patterson/NASIC effect) (BLS OEWS May 2023, 17-1021). By state, Texas has the most cartographers (1,740) and the highest concentrations are in public-land and energy states (Wyoming LQ 6.06, Montana 5.84, Colorado 4.57, Idaho 3.49, Oregon 3.34), while May 2025 state medians are highest in California ($102,780), Minnesota ($99,850), Washington ($96,100), Nevada and Massachusetts and lowest in Illinois ($54,680) (BLS OEWS May 2023; TradeCareerPath republishing BLS May 2025). Surveyors cluster in Houston (1,570), New York, Boston, Washington, Atlanta and Dallas, and every one of the ten best-paying surveyor metros is in California (BLS OEWS May 2023, 17-1022). Geographers are in Washington (280, LQ 9.54, $108,250), Austin (100, LQ 8.17), Baltimore and Denver (BLS OEWS May 2023, 19-3092).

Credentials and skills move pay more than geography does within this field. GISP holders earned a mean of $105,778 versus $85,103 for non-GISPs in the 2024 URISA/GPN survey, a 24% premium that is partly a seniority effect; cleared professionals averaged $119,131 in total compensation in 2025 (polygraph holders $141,299), with Virginia ($131,612), Maryland ($130,878) and Colorado ($126,936) the top-paying states (GISCI Salary Information; BusinessWire/ClearanceJobs). ArcGIS appears in 75% of 2025 GIS postings, Python in 34%, SQL in 22% and JavaScript in 13%, and ONET flags ArcGIS, SQL, AWS and AutoCAD as "in demand" (Geoawesome, Aug 2026; O*NET). On AI, the consensus among practitioners is that manual digitization, first-pass image classification and templated reporting are the exposed tasks, while hybrid GIS-plus-coding, data-engineering, cloud and MLOps roles are growing; the official projections, which still show positive growth for cartographers and surveyors, contain no statistical evidence of net displacement yet (GeoSpatial Training Services; Geoawesome). Entry-level reality is the hard part: PayScale's entry-level GIS specialist figure is about $48,600* (self-reported, last updated 2023), close to the 10th percentile of the cartographer distribution ($53,740), and surveying offers by far the most openings per incumbent (about 6% a year) for those willing to pursue licensure (PayScale; TradeCareerPath).

Federal reshuffle moves GEOINT work from Washington to St. Louis and Huntsville

The single most important 2025–26 development for geospatial careers is a geographic one. NGA's $1.7 billion Next NGA West campus opened on October 8, 2025 in north St. Louis, the largest federal investment in the city's history, with about 3,150 NGA employees moving from the old Soulard site; NGA leadership said the project stayed on schedule "despite budget pressures and workforce reductions" (Expansion Solutions Magazine; ClearanceJobs). Around it, Greater St. Louis Inc. values the regional geospatial economy at nearly $5 billion and about 27,000 jobs across the 15-county bi-state region, and in February 2026 it launched GeoSTL, a nonprofit led by former NGA chief AI officer Mark Munsell to build employer-driven apprenticeships and school-to-job pathways for "thousands of geospatial roles expected in the coming years" (Greater St. Louis Inc.; Hoodline). The contractor layer is already there: GDIT's geospatial innovation center at Cortex, the Globe Building (the largest multi-tenant SCIF outside Washington), T-REX's Geospatial Innovation Center, and the Taylor Geospatial Institute, an eight-university consortium (SLU, WashU, UMSL, Mizzou, Missouri S&T, Harris-Stowe, Illinois and the Danforth Center) that became a standalone nonprofit in 2025 (Expansion Solutions; SLU, May 2025). NGA's commercial-imagery vehicles sustain a national contractor base as well: the Luno B IDIQ (January 2025, $200 million ceiling) went to 13 firms including Booz Allen, BAE, CACI, BlackSky, Maxar Mission Solutions, NV5 Geospatial, Planet Labs Federal and Ursa Space, and delivery orders continued through 2025 (Via Satellite).

Huntsville is the other confirmed gainer. The President announced on September 2, 2025 that US Space Command headquarters will move from Colorado Springs to Redstone Arsenal, which already hosts about 46,000 federal employees and an FBI presence of 2,000-plus that is set to double; Huntsville's mayor cited about 1,400 direct jobs over five years, roughly 700 of them to be filled locally, with a temporary facility in 18–24 months and a permanent one in five to seven years (WAFF; Military.com; WWNO). The FBI ordered about 500 Washington staff to Redstone in February 2025, and UAH's Earth System Science program holds an NGA/USGS Center of Academic Excellence designation with ties to NASA's SERVIR and DEVELOP programs (WAFF; UAH News). The offset is a planned 5–8% Pentagon civilian cut that also touches Huntsville (WWNO).

The civilian science side contracted and dispersed. NOAA lost about 2,000 of 12,000 staff by March 2025 with 1,000-plus more layoffs planned; Interior's May 2025 RIF plan targeted about 1,000 USGS positions alongside a hiring freeze, and the FY2026 budget proposal sought to cut USGS by $562 million and restructure Landsat Next, clouding the outlook for the EROS center in Sioux Falls (ABC13; Nextgov; Kiowa County Press). USDA announced in July 2025 that it will cut its Washington-area workforce from 4,600 to about 2,000 and relocate thousands of staff to five hubs, Raleigh, Kansas City, Indianapolis, Fort Collins and Salt Lake City, with NRCS aligning to those hubs and the Forest Service closing its regional offices (GovExec). Contractors felt it too: Booz Allen cut 7% of its workforce in May 2025, called its civil business "the most challenging market in a generation," and guided civil revenue down by low-20s percent while describing defense and intelligence as "much healthier" (Washington Technology). That contrast, civil down and defense-intel stable, is the clearest employer-side signal in the notes.

The commercial map is stable rather than expanding. Esri (5,500 employees, Redlands CA) shows no layoff evidence; Maxar Intelligence rebranded as Vantor (Westminster CO, 1,909 employees) and pivoted toward defense products; Trimble is consolidated in Westminster; Woolpert (Dayton, 3,000-plus staff) remains a top-40 ENR design firm; Planet (San Francisco) cut 17% in 2024 and has been quiet since; and BlackSky grew revenue 50% in Q2 2026 on international customers while US-government revenue was flat (Esri Fact Sheet; Wikipedia: Vantor; Woolpert; GuruFocus on BlackSky). Colorado has two negatives on the ledger: Palantir moved its headquarters from Denver to Miami in February 2026, leaving about 500 Colorado staff with an uncertain future, and Colorado Springs loses Space Command (Denver Gazette). Important commercial segments (Hexagon, HERE, Mapbox, autonomous-vehicle mapping, utility and telecom GIS, insurance climate-risk, ag-tech, energy-sector GIS in Houston and Oklahoma City) were not researched and are a genuine blind spot in this report.

The education pipeline is also spatially specific, and for a Missouri student it is unusually favorable. Penn State World Campus and Arizona State's online program are the largest credential producers nationally, so alumni are dispersed; place-anchored talent pools form where campus programs meet federal demand, in Washington/Northern Virginia (George Mason, Maryland, Johns Hopkins, Virginia Tech, all USGIF-accredited), St. Louis, Seattle, the Triangle and the Front Range (Data USA; USGIF Education). Missouri has three NGA-linked on-ramps: Mizzou's USGIF-accredited GEOINT certificates and Center for Geospatial Intelligence, which holds one of only three formal NGA Educational Partnership Agreements nationally and sends students to NGA summer internships; UMSL's own NGA partnership agreement and Geospatial Collaborative; and Northwest Missouri State's paid NGA topographic-map production program, which has hired 15 student cartographers since 2022 (Mizzou Geography; NGA–UMSL release; Northwest Missouri State). Missouri State's B.S. in Geospatial Sciences and GIS graduate certificate are documented, but no Missouri State–NGA agreement and no TGI membership were found, so a Missouri State student's practical route into the St. Louis pipeline runs through GeoSTL apprenticeships, GeoFutures Week and Geo-Resolution events, or graduate study at a TGI member (Missouri State/OTC guide; Greater St. Louis Inc., GeoFutures).

St. Louis ranks first for a Missouri graduate because it is the only metro in the country where a federal GEOINT anchor is growing, the civic infrastructure to hire is new and explicit, and housing is cheap: the home-price-to-cartographer-wage ratio of 3.7 is the best of any hub except the tiny Dayton niche, rent is 18% below the national median and the HSH income needed to buy the median home is $75,024 versus $158,875 in Washington (HSH Q1 2026). Two cautions apply. The OEWS count of 50 cartographers is a severe undercount because NGA analysts are coded under other occupations and federal employment is often suppressed, so the published St. Louis wage reflects the civilian market, where PayScale's self-reported GIS analyst figure is only about $50,000 (14 profiles, 2023) (Payscale St. Louis). And the broader St. Louis economy is flat-to-shrinking in the 2026 county data, so the opportunity is sector-specific and clearance-gated: NGA's N2W is a top-secret facility, and NGA staff were among those offered deferred resignations in 2025, so net federal hiring there is replacement-plus rather than a surge (ClearanceJobs; Yahoo News). A Missouri State student should start the clearance clock early, through an NGA internship via Mizzou or UMSL, a GeoSTL apprenticeship, or a contractor at Cortex or the Globe Building.

Denver and the Front Range rank second on sheer depth and pay: 660 cartographer jobs at an LQ of 5.12, a mean of $92,780 that leaves the highest post-rent income of any metro ($73,424), the densest commercial EO and GNSS cluster (Vantor, Trimble, Lanteris and multiple Luno awardees), Colorado's $126,936 average cleared compensation, and a USDA hub plus USGS and BLM presence in Fort Collins and Lakewood for natural-resource GIS (BLS OEWS Denver; Wikipedia: Vantor; GovExec). Orbyt's April 2026 Denver GIS-analyst median of $81,000 (+5% year over year) corroborates the pay level, though its 1.08x cost-of-living multiplier trims the adjusted figure to about $75,000 (Orbyt Jobs). The reasons it is not first are the 2026 headwinds: Denver County employment fell 1.3% to March 2026, Palantir took its headquarters to Miami, Space Command is leaving Colorado Springs, Axios reports Colorado aerospace growth slowing, and the home-price-to-wage ratio of 6.9 is nearly double St. Louis's (BLS QCEW; Denver Gazette). Treat Colorado as stable, deep and expensive rather than expanding.

Washington DC and Northern Virginia rank third, with a hard condition: it is the right destination only for cleared or clearance-eligible candidates aiming at defense and intelligence work (NGA Springfield, NRO, the Luno and EOCL contractor set) or for spatial data scientists targeting its 33,515 AI jobs. It still has the largest geospatial employment base in the country (700 cartographers, 280 geographers, 1,330 surveyors) and the highest cleared pay (Virginia $131,612), and Loudoun County's data-center boom (+2.8% employment) is a bright spot (BLS OEWS May 2023, 17-1021; BusinessWire/ClearanceJobs). But for civil-agency GIS it is the worst large market in America right now: the metro lost 66,000 jobs, DC and Arlington each shed 4.5% of county employment, USDA is moving thousands of positions out, Booz Allen's civil business is in a generational slump, and a new graduate would compete against thousands of displaced experienced federal and contractor staff while paying $2,174 in rent (BLS Metro; BLS QCEW; Washington Technology).

Huntsville ranks fourth as the highest-momentum, lowest-cost defense option. Its population grew 2.6% (sixth-fastest metro), unemployment was about 2%, rent is the lowest of any hub at $1,004, the tech wage averages $123,142, and Space Command, the FBI and NGA's GEOHuntsville presence give it multiple inbound federal flows (Census Bureau; Apartment List Huntsville; Axios Huntsville). The caveats are that BLS publishes no cartographer wage for the metro (the market is thin outside defense), the surveyor mean is a low $58,610, PayScale's tiny-sample geospatial-analyst figure is only $41,787, and the Space Command jobs arrive over five to seven years, not next spring (BLS OEWS Huntsville; Payscale Huntsville; Military.com). It suits a candidate with a clearance path and an aerospace, remote-sensing or NASA (SERVIR/DEVELOP) orientation.

Raleigh-Durham ranks fifth as the best non-cleared growth market: the second-fastest-growing large metro by payrolls (+2.2%), Wake County +2.3%, population +2.4%, a USDA hub, NC State's Center for Geospatial Analytics with its paid I-GROUP internships, 240 cartographer jobs at an LQ of 4.15, and the third-best rent-to-tech-wage ratio in the country (BLS Metro; NC State CGA; CBRE). Its weakness is pay: the cartographer mean ($69,300) and Indeed's GIS-analyst average ($63,149) are the lowest of the major hubs, leaving post-rent income of $52,800 (Indeed). Charlotte, with +21,400 jobs and fast-growing suburbs, is a reasonable companion option in the same state.

Texas (Austin sixth, Houston seventh) offers the largest state-level geospatial employment (1,740 cartographers, 6,410 surveyors) inside the fastest-growing state economy (+154,300 jobs). Austin has 370 cartographer jobs, 100 geographers (LQ 8.17, state agencies), a top-five tech-talent score and a rent-to-tech-wage ratio of 12.7%; Houston has the largest surveyor base of any metro (1,570), the largest numeric metro job gain in the country (+44,700) and a home-price-to-wage ratio of 4.3 (BLS OEWS May 2023, 17-1021; BLS OEWS May 2023, 17-1022; BLS Metro). Both cores are flat in the county data (Travis +0.2%, Harris -0.6%) with growth in the suburbs, and Texas surveyor pay is below the national median ($61,150 versus $75,440), so Texas is a volume and engineering-services play rather than a pay play (TradeCareerPath Texas). Dallas-Fort Worth (330 cartographers, 29,840 AI jobs, UT Dallas USGIF accreditation) and San Antonio (the most affordable tech market, 12.3% rent-to-wage) are legitimate alternates.

Kansas City ranks eighth and is the better in-state alternative for a graduate who wants to stay near home without a clearance: its cartographer mean ($80,260) is actually higher than St. Louis's, post-rent income is $63,844, the home-price-to-wage ratio is 4.3, and USDA is moving thousands of positions to the metro, including NRCS (BLS OEWS Kansas City; GovExec). It has no identified GEOINT or commercial EO cluster, and Jackson County employment fell 0.7% to March 2026. Seattle (ninth) and the Bay Area (tenth) are for the spatial-data-science track only: they pay the most nominally (cartographers $95,370 and $117,630; data scientists $151,320 and $158,940), host Planet, the University of Washington pipeline and the largest AI workforces, and San Jose is one of the six large metros with significant job growth; but San Francisco rent ($3,878, up 25.7% in a year) consumes 39.6% of a cartographer wage and the home-price ratio of 11.5 is three times St. Louis's (BLS OEWS San Francisco; Apartment List San Francisco).

Several places deserve a note outside the top ten. Dayton has the fourth-highest cartographer wage in the nation ($101,280) and Woolpert's headquarters, but only 40 published jobs and a Wright-Patterson county (Greene) shedding 2.5% of employment; it is a niche for cleared photogrammetry, not a market (BLS OEWS Dayton). Minneapolis is underrated: Minnesota's May 2025 cartographer median ($99,850) is the second-highest in the country, the metro added 27,800 jobs, and the home-price ratio is a moderate 5.0, though Hennepin County employment fell 1.1% (TradeCareerPath; BLS Metro). Salt Lake City has the fastest large-metro payroll growth (+2.5%), a USDA hub and Northrop's 1,500-job Layton expansion, but a low cartographer mean ($67,620) against a home-price ratio of 8.5 (BLS OEWS Salt Lake City). Richmond VA tops Indeed's GIS-analyst pay list ($96,845), ranks second on LinkedIn's Cities on the Rise and grew 1.4% in county employment, likely on state-agency and utility demand (Indeed; LinkedIn News). Columbus/Licking County is the fastest-growing large county in America on the Intel and data-center build-out, which generates surveying and utility-GIS work, though no cartographer wage is published. Redlands/Riverside (Esri) pays $89,570 with post-rent income of $67,646 but a 6.7 home-price ratio. Places to avoid for now are Portland (330 cartographer jobs but metro payrolls -1.5% and Washington County -2.8%), Colorado Springs as a stand-alone destination (Space Command exit, county employment -1.2%), Chicago (Illinois has the lowest cartographer median of any state at $54,680 and rising unemployment), and the civil-agency side of Washington.

Springfield, Missouri itself is a thin market. BLS publishes no cartographer or geographer wage for the metro; its surveyor mean is $63,130, data scientists average $75,150 (about 180 jobs), software developers $96,760 (780 jobs) and the all-occupation mean is $52,110, while Greene County wages grew only 1.4% in the year to March 2026 (BLS OEWS Springfield MO; BLS QCEW). Housing is cheap (Zillow home value $243,597, average rent $1,192), so a local-government or engineering-firm GIS job there is livable, but it is not a place to build a geospatial career at scale (Zillow Springfield MO). Columbia is similar (surveyors $59,150, data scientists $84,170), with Mizzou's NGA internship pipeline as its main asset (BLS OEWS Columbia MO).

By career track, the recommendations resolve as follows. For cleared GEOINT and defense work: St. Louis, then Northern Virginia, Huntsville, Denver and Dayton. For spatial data science and GeoAI, where Python, SQL and cloud skills move a graduate from the roughly $75,000 analyst band into the $120,000 data-scientist band: the Bay Area, Seattle, Austin, Dallas-Fort Worth, Denver and Raleigh, with Minneapolis as a value option. For surveying and engineering-services work, the highest-volume entry path: Houston, Dallas, the Carolinas, Florida and Utah, with California for pay once licensed. For federal natural-resource and agricultural GIS: Fort Collins, Salt Lake City, Raleigh and Kansas City, the USDA hubs. For local-government GIS, the largest single employer of cartographers: fast-growing Sunbelt suburbs (Collin and Fort Bend TX, Wake and Cabarrus NC, Hillsborough FL, where a January 2026 senior GIS analyst posting paid $56,451–$77,646) (Hillsborough County).

What the data cannot tell you

Honesty about vintages is essential because the geospatial-specific geography rests on older data than the macro picture. The national labor-market figures are current (September 2026 payrolls and state data, August metro and JOLTS data, the October 14 Beige Book), and county employment is March 2026. But every metro-level geospatial employment count, location quotient and wage in this report is May 2023 OEWS, because the May 2024 and May 2025 BLS occupation-profile and metro pages now render through a JavaScript application and the bulk data files were blocked; national May 2025 medians come from the Occupational Outlook Handbook, and the national cartographer median rose 6.8% between the two vintages, so metro wages are probably 5–8% higher today than shown, which would not change the rankings because rent and home-price differences across metros are three to four times wider than wage differences (BLS OOH). The May 2025 state and metro tables exist at bls.gov/oes/2025/may and should be pulled before any final decision. Rents are October 2026 city-level medians from Apartment List, which can differ from metro medians; home prices are NAR Q1 2026 via HSH or Zillow city values of varying dates.

Three gaps are structural rather than vintage-related. First, no source provides 2025–26 US geospatial job-posting counts by metro, employer, seniority or remote share; the "20,000–25,000 openings" figure is an industry-association estimate with unstated methodology and unclear US-only scope, and the URISA/GPN 2024 salary survey's geographic cross-tabs are paywalled (Geo Week News; GPN). Second, the SOC taxonomy undercounts GIS work badly: analysts and developers are scattered across "computer occupations, all other," data scientists and technician codes, so the roughly 70,000 jobs in the three core codes are a floor, and federal GEOINT employment is largely invisible in metro OEWS tables (which is why St. Louis shows 50 cartographers next to 3,150 NGA employees). Third, large commercial segments were not researched at all: Hexagon, HERE, TomTom, Mapbox, the Google/Apple/Meta mapping teams, autonomous-vehicle mapping, utility and telecom GIS, insurance and climate-risk analytics, ag-tech (including Bayer/Climate in St. Louis and Deere) and energy-sector GIS in Houston and Oklahoma City. State and local government GIS program sizes and hiring are essentially undocumented beyond anecdotes. No 2026 headcount or hiring figures exist in the notes for NGA, Vantor, Esri, Planet or Trimble, and the scale of the October 2025 KBR/EROS WARN notice in Sioux Falls is unverified. The federal-policy sequence (September Fed hike, Warsh chairmanship, Fed cut in December 2025) relies on broker commentary and should be confirmed against primary Fed statements. Finally, the recommendations assume policy continuity: a reversal of the 2025 federal cuts would re-rank Washington upward, and a recession, which no retrieved forecaster calls the base case but which Goldman flags as the trigger for deferred AI-related cuts, would hit the tech-heavy coastal markets first and the clearance-gated federal hubs last.

Conclusion

The practical lesson of 2026 is that geospatial opportunity has decoupled from the general job market. The metros with the strongest overall growth (Houston, Salt Lake City, Raleigh, Charlotte, Baton Rouge) are not the metros with the strongest geospatial momentum (St. Louis, Huntsville), and the metro with the largest geospatial base (Washington) is the one large economy in contraction. Because half of cartographer jobs and two-thirds of geographer jobs are in government, the field's geography follows federal real-estate and reorganization decisions more than it follows population or GDP, and in 2025–26 those decisions moved work toward the interior: St. Louis, Huntsville, and the five USDA hubs in Raleigh, Kansas City, Indianapolis, Fort Collins and Salt Lake City. A Missouri graduate is, unusually, already standing near the center of the map.

The second lesson is that location choice and skill choice are the same decision. The pay distribution in this field is bimodal: a conventional GIS analyst in a local government or engineering firm earns $50,000–$80,000 almost anywhere, while a cleared GEOINT analyst or a geospatial data scientist earns $110,000–$140,000 in a handful of places. The clearance route runs through on-site federal nodes and is most accessible from Missouri via the NGA partnerships at Mizzou, UMSL and Northwest and the new GeoSTL apprenticeships; the data-science route runs through Python, SQL and cloud skills and leads to Austin, Denver, the Triangle or the coasts. In a low-hire market where postings draw hundreds of applicants in days, the scarce assets are not degrees, which the country produces 7,000 of a year, but the place-bound internships, clearances and employer relationships that only a few metros can confer, and St. Louis is the one of those metros where a Missouri student can get them cheapest.
